Some Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry

Classification, nomenclature, isomerism, and basic mechanisms underlying organic reactions.

▶

Classification of Organic Compounds

Organic compounds are broadly classified based on their structural framework.

1. Based on Structure

  • Open-chain (Acyclic) or Aliphatic: Straight or branched chains of carbon atoms. E.g., Butane, Isobutane.
  • Closed-chain (Cyclic) or Ring Compounds:
    • Homocyclic (Carbocyclic): Ring contains only carbon atoms. E.g., Cyclohexane, Benzene (aromatic).
    • Heterocyclic: Ring contains carbon and at least one other heteroatom (O, N, S). E.g., Pyridine, Furan.

2. Functional Groups Table

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms joined in a specific manner which determines the chemical properties of the organic compound.

3. Homologous Series

A series of organic compounds with the same functional group and similar chemical properties in which successive members differ by a -CH₂- group (14u mass).

🧬 NEET Tip
Physical properties (like boiling point) generally increase with molecular mass in a homologous series due to increased van der Waals forces, but chemical properties remain very similar.

⭐ Key Points

  • Organic compounds are classified into open-chain and closed-chain.
  • Functional groups dictate the chemical behavior of the molecule.
  • Homologous series differ by a CH₂ group.

IUPAC Nomenclature

A systematic method of naming organic chemical compounds as recommended by IUPAC.

Rules for Naming:

  1. Longest Chain Rule: Select the longest continuous carbon chain containing the principal functional group, multiple bonds, or maximum substituents.
  2. Numbering Rule: Number the chain to give the lowest possible locants to the principal functional group, then double/triple bonds, then substituents.
  3. Alphabetical Order: Prefix names of substituents are listed alphabetically.
  4. Format: Prefix(es) + Word root + Primary suffix (ane/ene/yne) + Secondary suffix (functional group).

Naming Examples

  • Alcohol: CH₃-CH(OH)-CH₃ is Propan-2-ol.
  • Aldehyde: CH₃-CH₂-CHO is Propanal.
  • Ketone: CH₃-CO-CH₂-CH₃ is Butan-2-one.
  • Carboxylic Acid: CH₃-CH(CH₃)-COOH is 2-Methylpropanoic acid.
  • Amine: CH₃-CH₂-NH₂ is Ethanamine.
  • Halide: CH₃-CHCl-CH₃ is 2-Chloropropane.
  • Ether: CH₃-O-CH₂-CH₃ is Methoxyethane.
⚠️ Common Mistake
When naming compounds with both double and triple bonds, double bonds get priority in numbering if both get the same locant from either end (e.g., But-1-en-3-yne, not But-3-en-1-yne).

⭐ Key Points

  • Find the longest chain containing the principal group.
  • Number to minimize locants.
  • Assemble using: prefix + root + primary suffix + secondary suffix.

Isomerism

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulas or spatial arrangements.

1. Structural Isomerism

Compounds with different bonding patterns.

  • Chain: Different carbon skeleton (e.g., n-butane and isobutane).
  • Position: Different position of functional group or multiple bond (e.g., butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol).
  • Functional Group: Different functional groups (e.g., ethanol and dimethyl ether).
  • Metamerism: Different alkyl chains on either side of a polyvalent functional group (e.g., diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether).
  • Tautomerism: Dynamic equilibrium between two isomers, usually involving a proton shift (e.g., keto-enol tautomerism).

2. Stereoisomerism

Compounds with same bonding but different spatial arrangement.

Geometrical Isomerism (ref)

Arises due to restricted rotation around a double bond or in a ring.

  • Cis: Similar groups on the same side.
  • Trans: Similar groups on opposite sides.
  • E/Z System: Used when 3 or 4 different groups are attached. Assign priorities using CIP rules. Z = high priority groups on same side (Zusammen), E = opposite (Entgegen).
Optical Isomerism (ref)

Compounds that rotate the plane of polarized light.

  • Chirality (ref): A molecule is chiral if it is non-superimposable on its mirror image (usually has an asymmetric carbon/chiral centre).
  • Enantiomers: Non-superimposable mirror images. Rotate light by equal amounts in opposite directions.
  • Diastereomers: Stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other.
  • Meso Compounds: Have chiral centres but are optically inactive due to an internal plane of symmetry.
R/S Configuration (CIP Rules)
  1. Assign priorities to the 4 groups on the chiral centre based on atomic number.
  2. View the molecule with the lowest priority group pointing away.
  3. If the sequence 1 → 2 → 3 is clockwise, it is R (Rectus). If anti-clockwise, it is S (Sinister).

⭐ Key Points

  • Structural isomers differ in bonding (chain, position, functional, metamerism, tautomerism).
  • Stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement (geometrical and optical).
  • Chirality implies non-superimposable mirror images; CIP rules assign E/Z and R/S.

Electronic Effects

Electronic effects permanently or temporarily alter the electron distribution in organic molecules, impacting stability and reactivity.

1. Inductive Effect (ref)

Permanent polarization of a σ bond due to differences in electronegativity. It operates through σ bonds and decreases rapidly with distance.

  • -I Effect (Electron Withdrawing): Draws electrons away. E.g., -NO₂ > -CN > -F > -Cl > -Br > -I > -OH > -NH₂
  • +I Effect (Electron Donating): Pushes electrons. E.g., -C(CH₃)₃ > -CH(CH₃)₂ > -CH₂CH₃ > -CH₃

2. Resonance / Mesomeric Effect (ref)

Delocalization of π electrons or lone pairs. Permanent effect.

  • +M / +R Effect: Donates electrons to the conjugated system via lone pair or π bond. E.g., -OH, -OR, -NH₂, -Cl.
  • -M / -R Effect: Withdraws electrons from the conjugated system. E.g., -NO₂, -CN, -CHO, -COOH.

3. Hyperconjugation (ref)

Delocalization of σ electrons of an α-C-H bond into an adjacent empty or partially filled p-orbital or π-orbital. Also called "no-bond resonance".

More α-hydrogens = more hyperconjugative structures = greater stability.

4. Electromeric Effect

Temporary, complete transfer of π electrons to one of the bonded atoms in the presence of an attacking reagent.

  • +E: Transfer to the atom where reagent attacks.
  • -E: Transfer away from the atom where reagent attacks.

Stability of Intermediates

IntermediateStabilized ByOrder of Stability
Carbocation (C⁺)+I, +M, Hyperconjugation3° > 2° > 1° > Methyl⁺
Free Radical (C•)+I, +M, Hyperconjugation3° > 2° > 1° > Methyl•
Carbanion (C⁻)-I, -MMethyl⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°
📝 Note
Resonance generally dominates over hyperconjugation, which dominates over the inductive effect (R > H > I), except for halogens where -I > +M.

⭐ Key Points

  • Inductive effect acts via σ bonds; depends on electronegativity.
  • Resonance/Mesomeric effect involves π/lone pair delocalization.
  • Hyperconjugation involves σ-C-H bond delocalization.
  • Stability orders: Carbocation (3°>2°>1°), Radical (3°>2°>1°), Carbanion (1°>2°>3°).

Reaction Mechanisms

A reaction mechanism is the step-by-step description of bond breaking and bond making in a chemical reaction.

1. Bond Fission

  • Homolysis (ref): Symmetrical breaking of a covalent bond. Each atom takes one electron. Forms free radicals. Promoted by HEAT, LIGHT, or PEROXIDES.
  • Heterolysis (ref): Unsymmetrical breaking. More electronegative atom takes both electrons. Forms carbocations and carbanions.

2. Types of Reagents

  • Nucleophiles (Nu⁻): "Nucleus loving". Electron-rich species that attack electron-deficient centers. E.g., OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O.
  • Electrophiles (E⁺): "Electron loving". Electron-deficient species that attack electron-rich centers. E.g., H⁺, NO₂⁺, AlCl₃, BF₃.

3. Types of Organic Reactions

  • Substitution: Replacement of an atom/group by another. Can be Nucleophilic (SN1, SN2), Electrophilic (EAS), or Free Radical.
  • Addition: Reagent adds to a multiple bond (no byproducts). Usually Electrophilic (alkenes/alkynes) or Nucleophilic (carbonyls).
  • Elimination: Removal of atoms/groups to form a multiple bond (usually forms byproducts like H₂O or HX).
  • Rearrangement: Migration of an atom/group within a molecule to form a more stable isomer (e.g., carbocation rearrangement).

4. Arrow Notation

  • Curved Arrow: Shows movement of an electron pair. Starts at a lone pair or bond, ends at the atom receiving the pair.
  • Fish-hook Arrow (Half-headed): Shows movement of a single electron (in homolysis).

⭐ Key Points

  • Homolysis yields free radicals; heterolysis yields ions.
  • Nucleophiles are electron-rich; Electrophiles are electron-deficient.
  • Major reaction types: Substitution, Addition, Elimination, Rearrangement.

Hydrocarbons

Alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic compounds: preparation, properties, and reactions.

▶

Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated open-chain hydrocarbons containing only carbon-carbon single bonds. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

Preparation

  • Decarboxylation: Sodium salts of carboxylic acids heated with soda lime (NaOH + CaO) yield alkanes containing one carbon less.
    R-COONa + NaOH → CaO, Δ R-H + Na₂CO₃
  • Hydrogenation: Addition of H₂ to alkenes/alkynes in presence of finely divided Ni, Pd, or Pt.
🔬 Reaction: Wurtz Reaction
Overall: 2 R-X + 2 Na → R-R + 2 NaX
R-X + 2 Na (dry ether)
Step 1: Reaction of Alkyl Halide with Sodium
Alkyl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to form higher symmetrical alkanes.
Why? The active metal Na donates electrons to form a radical or carbanion intermediate, coupling the alkyl groups. Best for forming symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbon atoms.
Step 1 of 1

Chemical Reactions

Alkanes are generally inert but undergo substitution reactions under special conditions.

🔬 Reaction: Free Radical Halogenation
Overall: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (in presence of UV light / hν)
Cl-Cl → hν 2 Cl•
Step 1: Initiation
UV light causes homolytic cleavage of the Cl-Cl bond.
Why? The Cl-Cl bond is relatively weak and can be cleaved by photons of appropriate energy, creating highly reactive chlorine free radicals.
Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃•
CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
Step 2: Propagation
The chlorine radical abstracts a hydrogen from methane, creating a methyl radical. This methyl radical then attacks a Cl₂ molecule, creating the product and another Cl•.
Why? This is a chain reaction. Radicals are unstable and seek to pair their electrons. The newly formed radical perpetuates the cycle.
Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆
CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl
Step 3: Termination
Any two radicals combine to form a stable molecule.
Why? The chain reaction stops when radicals consume each other, terminating the chain propagation.
Step 1 of 3
🧬 NEET Tip
In halogenation, reactivity order of halogens is F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Reactivity order of hydrogens is 3° > 2° > 1°. Selectivity for bromination is much higher than for chlorination.

⭐ Key Points

  • Wurtz reaction doubles the alkyl chain (best for symmetrical alkanes).
  • Free radical substitution (halogenation) follows initiation, propagation, termination.

Alkenes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ.

Preparation

  • Dehydration of Alcohols: Heating alcohols with conc. H₂SO₄. Follows Saytzeff Rule: Highly substituted alkene is major product.
  • Dehydrohalogenation of Alkyl Halides: Heating with alcoholic KOH (beta-elimination). Also follows Saytzeff rule.

Addition Reactions

🔬 Reaction: Addition of HX (Markownikoff's Rule)
Overall: Propene + HBr → 2-Bromopropane
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃-CH⁺-CH₃ (2° carbocation)
Step 1: Electrophilic Attack by H⁺
The π electrons of the alkene attack the H⁺ from HBr. This forms a more stable secondary carbocation.
Why? Markownikoff's rule states the negative part goes to the carbon with fewer hydrogens. Mechanistically, this is because the reaction proceeds via the most stable intermediate (2° carbocation is more stable than 1° due to hyperconjugation).
CH₃-CH⁺-CH₃ + Br⁻ → CH₃-CHBr-CH₃
Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack by Br⁻
The bromide ion (Br⁻) attacks the positive carbon of the intermediate carbocation to form the final product.
Why? The nucleophile completes the addition by forming a bond with the electron-deficient carbocation.
Step 1 of 2
🔬 Reaction: Peroxide Effect (Anti-Markownikoff)
Overall: Propene + HBr (with Peroxides) → 1-Bromopropane
R-O-O-R → 2 R-O•
R-O• + HBr → R-OH + Br•
Step 1: Generation of Bromine Radical
Peroxides undergo homolytic cleavage. The resulting alkoxy radical abstracts a hydrogen from HBr, creating a bromine free radical.
Why? Peroxides are unstable and act as radical initiators. Note: This effect is ONLY observed with HBr, not HCl or HI.
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + Br• → CH₃-C•H-CH₂Br (2° radical)
Step 2: Addition of Br Radical
The Br• adds to the terminal carbon to form the more stable secondary free radical.
Why? Anti-Markownikoff addition. The reaction proceeds via the most stable free radical intermediate (2° radical > 1° radical).
CH₃-C•H-CH₂Br + HBr → CH₃-CH₂-CH₂Br + Br•
Step 3: Abstraction of Hydrogen
The secondary radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from another HBr molecule, completing the product and regenerating a Br• to continue the chain.
Why? This completes the chain propagation cycle, yielding the 1-bromo product.
Step 1 of 3

Ozonolysis: Alkene + O₃ followed by Zn/H₂O cleaves the double bond to form carbonyl compounds (aldehydes/ketones).

⭐ Key Points

  • Electrophilic addition of HX follows Markownikoff's rule (via carbocation).
  • Peroxide effect applies ONLY to HBr, resulting in anti-Markownikoff addition (via free radical).
  • Ozonolysis is a powerful tool to determine the position of a double bond.

Alkynes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon triple bond. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.

Preparation

  • From Calcium Carbide: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂ (Ethyne).
  • Dehydrohalogenation of vicinal dihalides: Requires strong bases.
    Step 1: Alc. KOH removes one HX.
    Step 2: NaNH₂ (stronger base) removes the second HX.

Properties & Reactions

  • Acidity of Terminal Alkynes: The sp hybridized carbon has 50% s-character, making it highly electronegative. The C-H bond is polar, and the proton can be easily removed by strong bases like NaNH₂.
  • Addition of H₂O (Hydration): Occurs in the presence of Hg²⁺/H⁺. Initially forms an enol, which undergoes tautomerization to form a more stable ketone (or acetaldehyde from ethyne).
  • Addition of HX: Follows Markownikoff's rule. With 2 moles of HX, geminal dihalides are formed.
  • Hydrogenation:
    • Lindlar's Catalyst (Pd/CaCO₃, poisoned with quinoline) stops at cis-alkene.
    • Birch Reduction (Na in liquid NH₃) yields trans-alkene.

⭐ Key Points

  • Terminal alkynes are acidic due to sp hybridization.
  • Hydration of alkynes yields ketones (except ethyne → acetaldehyde) via keto-enol tautomerization.
  • Stereoselective hydrogenation: Lindlar's gives cis, Birch gives trans.

Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Aromatic compounds (arenes) are highly stable cyclic compounds. Benzene (C₆H₆) is the parent compound.

Aromaticity (Hückel's Rule)

For a compound to be aromatic, it must be:

  1. Cyclic and planar.
  2. Completely conjugated (uninterrupted π cloud).
  3. Contain (4n + 2) π electrons, where n = 0, 1, 2...

Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS)

Because the benzene ring is electron-rich, it undergoes substitution by electrophiles while maintaining its stable aromatic system.

🔬 Reaction: EAS Halogenation (Bromination)
Overall: Benzene + Br₂ (with FeBr₃) → Bromobenzene + HBr
Br₂ + FeBr₃ → Br⁺ + FeBr₄⁻
Step 1: Generation of Electrophile
FeBr₃ acts as a Lewis acid and accepts a lone pair from Br₂, polarizing the bond and creating a strong bromonium ion electrophile (Br⁺).
Why? Benzene's π cloud is stable. A weak electrophile (like neutral Br₂) won't react. A Lewis acid catalyst is required to generate a strong electrophile.
C₆H₆ + Br⁺ → C₆H₆Br⁺ (Sigma Complex / Arenium Ion)
Step 2: Electrophilic Attack
Two π electrons from the benzene ring attack the Br⁺, forming a resonance-stabilized carbocation (sigma complex). Aromaticity is temporarily lost.
Why? The electron-rich ring is attracted to the positive electrophile.
C₆H₆Br⁺ + FeBr₄⁻ → C₆H₅Br + HBr + FeBr₃
Step 3: Loss of Proton (Rearomatization)
A base (FeBr₄⁻) removes a proton from the sp³ hybridized carbon. The electrons form a new π bond, restoring aromaticity.
Why? Regaining the massive stability of the aromatic system drives this final step. The catalyst (FeBr₃) is regenerated.
Step 1 of 3
🔬 Reaction: EAS Nitration
Overall: Benzene + HNO₃/H₂SO₄ → Nitrobenzene + H₂O
HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ ⇌ H₂NO₃⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → NO₂⁺ + H₂O + HSO₄⁻
Step 1: Generation of Nitronium Ion
Sulfuric acid (stronger acid) protonates nitric acid, which then loses water to form the nitronium ion (NO₂⁺) electrophile.
Why? The mixed acid generates a very strong electrophile needed to break benzene's stability.
C₆H₆ + NO₂⁺ → C₆H₆NO₂⁺
Step 2: Formation of Sigma Complex
The benzene ring attacks the NO₂⁺, losing aromaticity and forming the resonance-stabilized intermediate.
Why? The electron-rich ring seeks the strong positive charge on the nitrogen.
C₆H₆NO₂⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂SO₄
Step 3: Rearomatization
HSO₄⁻ removes the proton, restoring the aromatic ring to form nitrobenzene.
Why? Restoring aromatic stability. H₂SO₄ acts as a catalyst and is regenerated.
Step 1 of 3
🔬 Reaction: Friedel-Crafts Reactions
Overall: Alkylation (R-Cl) or Acylation (R-COCl) with AlCl₃
R-Cl + AlCl₃ → R⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ (Alkylation)
R-COCl + AlCl₃ → R-C≡O⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ (Acylation)
Step 1: Generation of Electrophile
The Lewis acid AlCl₃ abstracts a chloride ion to form a carbocation (alkylation) or acylium ion (acylation).
Why? To generate a strong carbon-based electrophile capable of attacking the aromatic ring. Note: In alkylation, the carbocation can undergo rearrangement to a more stable form before attacking!
Benzene + R⁺ → Arenium Ion → Substituted Benzene + H⁺
Step 2 & 3: Attack and Rearomatization
The aromatic ring attacks the carbocation/acylium ion, forms the sigma complex, and then loses a proton to restore aromaticity.
Why? Standard EAS mechanism driven by the ultimate restoration of aromatic stability.
Step 1 of 2

⭐ Key Points

  • Aromatic compounds must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated, and follow Hückel's Rule (4n+2).
  • They primarily undergo Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) to preserve the stable π system.
  • Activating groups direct ortho/para; deactivating groups direct meta (except halogens which are o/p directing but deactivating).

Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Preparation, properties, and important nucleophilic substitution mechanisms.

▶

Classification & Nomenclature

Haloalkanes and haloarenes are classified on the basis of the number of halogen atoms and the hybridization of the carbon atom to which they are bonded.

Based on Number of Halogen Atoms

  • Monohalo: Contain one halogen atom (e.g., CH3CH2X)
  • Dihalo: Contain two halogen atoms (e.g., CH2X2)
  • Polyhalo: Contain multiple halogen atoms (tri, tetra, etc.)

Based on sp³ C-X Bond

  • Alkyl Halides (R-X): X attached to alkyl group. Classified as 1°, 2°, or 3° based on the carbon holding the halogen.
  • Allylic Halides: X attached to sp³ carbon adjacent to a C=C double bond.
  • Benzylic Halides: X attached to sp³ carbon next to an aromatic ring.

Based on sp² C-X Bond

  • Vinylic Halides: X attached directly to one of the carbon atoms of a C=C double bond.
  • Aryl Halides: X attached directly to an aromatic ring.
📝 IUPAC Naming Rules
  1. Select the longest carbon chain containing the halogen.
  2. Number to give the lowest locant to the halogen.
  3. Name halogens as substituents: fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo.

⭐ Key Points

  • Alkyl halides are 1°, 2°, or 3° depending on the carbon atom.
  • Vinylic and aryl halides involve sp² hybridized carbon holding the halogen.
  • Common names exist like chloroform, bromoform, etc.
▶

Methods of Preparation

Haloalkanes can be prepared from alcohols, hydrocarbons, and by halogen exchange.

1. From Alcohols

  • Using HX: R-OH + HX → R-X + H2O. Reactivity: HI > HBr > HCl. For primary/secondary, ZnCl2 (Lucas reagent) is used with HCl.
  • Using Phosphorus Halides: 3R-OH + PCl3 → 3R-Cl + H3PO3.
  • Darzen's Process: Using SOCl2. This is the preferred method!
🔬 Reaction: Darzen's Process
Overall: R-OH + SOCl₂ → R-Cl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑
R-OH + SOCl₂ → R-O-SOCl + HCl
Step 1: Formation of Alkyl Chlorosulfite
The alcohol reacts with thionyl chloride to form an alkyl chlorosulfite intermediate.
Why? Oxygen acts as a nucleophile and attacks the electrophilic sulfur.
R-O-SOCl → R-Cl + SO₂↑
Step 2: Decomposition
The intermediate decomposes to give the alkyl chloride.
Why? The formation of highly stable SO₂ gas drives the reaction forward. Since both byproducts (SO₂ and HCl) are gases, they escape, leaving pure alkyl chloride!
Step 1 of 2

2. From Hydrocarbons

  • Free Radical Halogenation: Alkanes + X2 (UV light) → mixture of isomeric haloalkanes.
  • Electrophilic Substitution: Arenes + X2 (Fe or FeCl3) → Aryl halides.

3. Halide Exchange

  • Finkelstein Reaction: R-Cl/Br + NaI → R-I + NaCl/NaBr (in dry acetone). Precipitates NaCl/NaBr.
  • Swarts Reaction: Synthesis of alkyl fluorides. R-Cl/Br + AgF/Hg2F2/CoF3/SbF3 → R-F.

⭐ Key Points

  • SOCl2 is best for alkyl chlorides because byproducts are gases.
  • Finkelstein is for alkyl iodides (NaI in acetone).
  • Swarts is for alkyl fluorides.
▶

Physical Properties

  • Boiling Points: R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F. BP increases with molecular mass. For isomeric haloalkanes, BP decreases with branching (due to reduced surface area and van der Waals forces).
  • Density: Bromo, iodo, and polychloro derivatives are heavier than water. Density increases with number of carbon atoms, halogen atoms, and atomic mass of halogen.
  • Solubility: Insoluble in water despite being polar, because they cannot form H-bonds with water and breaking existing H-bonds requires more energy. Soluble in organic solvents.
  • Dipole Moment: Usually CH3Cl > CH3F > CH3Br > CH3I. Fluorine is more electronegative, but Cl has a larger size, making its dipole moment slightly higher.

⭐ Key Points

  • Branching lowers boiling point.
  • Insoluble in water.
  • CH3Cl has a higher dipole moment than CH3F.

Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions (SN1 and SN2)

A nucleophile replaces the halogen atom. These are classified into SN1 and SN2 mechanisms.

🔬 Reaction: SN2 Mechanism
Overall: CH₃Br + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Br⁻
HO⁻ + CH₃-Br → [HO---CH₃---Br]⁻
Step 1: Backside Attack & Transition State
The nucleophile (OH⁻) attacks from the opposite side of the leaving group (Br).
Why? The front side is blocked by the bulky, electron-rich Br atom (steric hindrance). A transition state is formed with partial bonds.
[HO---CH₃---Br]⁻ → HO-CH₃ + Br⁻
Step 2: Walden Inversion
The C-Br bond breaks as the C-OH bond fully forms, inverting the configuration like an umbrella blowing inside out.
Why? The energy drops as stable products form. This one-step concerted process results in 100% inversion of configuration.
Step 1 of 2
🔬 Reaction: SN1 Mechanism
Overall: (CH₃)₃C-Br + H₂O → (CH₃)₃C-OH + HBr
(CH₃)₃C-Br ⇌ (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻
Step 1: Ionisation (Slow, Rate Determining)
The C-Br bond undergoes slow heterolysis to form a stable carbocation and bromide ion.
Why? The resulting 3° carbocation is stabilized by hyperconjugation and inductive effects. A polar protic solvent assists by solvating the ions.
(CH₃)₃C⁺ + H₂O → (CH₃)₃C-OH₂⁺ → (CH₃)₃C-OH + H⁺
Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack (Fast)
The nucleophile (H₂O) attacks the planar carbocation from either side.
Why? The carbocation is sp² hybridized (planar), so attack from top or bottom is equally likely, leading to racemisation (if chiral).

Reactions of Haloalkanes

  • Aqueous KOH: Yields Alcohols (SN)
  • Alcoholic KOH: Yields Alkenes (Elimination)

    • KCN (ionic) → Alkyl Cyanides (R-CN). C attacks.
    • AgCN (covalent) → Alkyl Isocyanides (R-NC). N attacks.

⭐ Key Points

  • SN2 involves backside attack, causing inversion. Favored by 1° halides.
  • SN1 involves carbocation formation, causing racemisation. Favored by 3° halides.
  • Good leaving group (like I⁻) increases the rate of both SN1 and SN2.

Elimination Reactions

When heated with alcoholic KOH, haloalkanes undergo β-elimination (dehydrohalogenation) to form alkenes.

🔬 Reaction: E1 Mechanism
Overall: (CH₃)₃C-Br + Base → CH₂=C(CH₃)₂ + HBr
(CH₃)₃C-Br ⇌ (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻
Step 1: Carbocation Formation
Similar to SN1, the leaving group leaves to form a carbocation.
Why? The tertiary carbocation is highly stable.
Base removes β-H from (CH₃)₃C⁺ → C=C
Step 2: Deprotonation
A weak base abstracts a proton from a β-carbon, forming a double bond.
Why? Instead of acting as a nucleophile, the base takes a proton, which is driven by heat and stability of the alkene.
Step 1 of 2
🔬 Reaction: E2 Mechanism
Overall: Base + R-CH₂-CH₂-Br → R-CH=CH₂ + Base-H + Br⁻
B:⁻ + H-C(β)-C(α)-Br → [Transition State] → C=C
Step 1: Concerted Elimination
The base removes the β-proton as the double bond forms and the leaving group departs, all in one step.
Why? Requires strong base. Must be anti-periplanar (H and Br on opposite sides) to minimize steric hindrance and allow orbital overlap.
Step 1 of 1

Saytzeff's Rule

In dehydrohalogenation reactions, the preferred product is that alkene which has the greater number of alkyl groups attached to the doubly bonded carbon atoms (more substituted alkene is more stable).

Substitution vs Elimination

  • SN1 vs E1: Heat favors E1. Both go through carbocations.
  • SN2 vs E2: Bulky base (like t-BuO⁻) favors E2. Strong bases favor E2. 3° halides always give E2 with strong bases. Aqueous conditions favor SN2, alcoholic conditions favor E2.

⭐ Key Points

  • Elimination requires heating and alcoholic KOH.
  • Saytzeff's rule: More highly substituted alkene is the major product.
  • E2 requires anti-periplanar geometry.

Polyhalogen Compounds

  • Dichloromethane (CH2Cl2): Used as a solvent, paint remover. Harms CNS.
  • Chloroform (CHCl3): Used as a solvent. Forms poisonous gas phosgene (COCl2) when exposed to air and light (stored in dark bottles).
  • Carbon Tetrachloride (CCl4): Used in fire extinguishers (Pyrene). Causes ozone depletion.
  • Iodoform (CHI3): Yellow solid, used as a mild antiseptic due to liberation of free I2.
  • Freons (CFCs): e.g., CCl2F2 (Freon-12). Used as refrigerants. Cause severe ozone depletion.
  • DDT: p,p'-Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane. Powerful insecticide but banned due to non-biodegradability and biological magnification.

⭐ Key Points

  • Chloroform is stored in dark bottles to prevent phosgene formation.
  • CFCs and CCl4 deplete the ozone layer.
  • DDT is highly stable and fat-soluble, leading to accumulation in the food chain.

Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

Preparation, properties and reactions of oxygen-containing functional groups.

▶

Classification & Nomenclature

Alcohols & Phenols Classification

  • Mono, Di, Trihydric: Contain one, two, or three -OH groups respectively.
  • Alcohols (1°, 2°, 3°): Based on the sp³ carbon holding the -OH group.
  • Allylic & Benzylic: -OH attached to sp³ C next to a C=C or benzene ring.
  • Vinylic & Phenols: -OH directly on sp² C.
📝 Naming
  1. Alcohols: Alkane - 'e' + 'ol' = Alkanol.
  2. Phenols: Derivatives of phenol. Common names: cresol (methylphenol), catechol (1,2-benzenediol), resorcinol (1,3-benzenediol), hydroquinone (1,4-benzenediol).
  3. Ethers: Alkoxyalkanes. The smaller alkyl group gets the 'oxy'.

⭐ Key Points

  • Alcohols are sp³ C-OH, Phenols are sp²(aromatic) C-OH.
  • Ethers are R-O-R'.
▶

Preparation of Alcohols

1. From Alkenes

  • Acid-Catalysed Hydration: Follows Markownikoff's rule. (H₂O/H⁺). Carbocation intermediate.
  • Hydroboration-Oxidation: Follows anti-Markownikoff addition (B₂H₆ then H₂O₂/OH⁻). No rearrangement, pure alcohols formed.

2. From Carbonyl Compounds

  • Reduction: Aldehydes → 1° Alcohols. Ketones → 2° Alcohols. Using H₂/Pd, NaBH₄, or LiAlH₄.
  • From Carboxylic Acids/Esters: Reduced by strong LiAlH₄ to 1° alcohols.

3. From Grignard Reagents

🔬 Reaction: Grignard Synthesis of Alcohols
Overall: R-MgX + >C=O → >C(R)-OH
R⁻ δ- -- Mg⁺X δ+ + C=O δ+ δ- → R-C-O⁻MgX⁺
Step 1: Nucleophilic Addition
The nucleophilic R group of Grignard attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon.
Why? Grignard reagent acts as a carbanion source. The carbonyl C is partially positive.
R-C-O⁻MgX⁺ + H₂O → R-C-OH + Mg(OH)X
Step 2: Hydrolysis
The magnesium alkoxide intermediate is hydrolyzed to form the alcohol.
Why? The O-Mg bond is ionic and easily broken by water/acid to yield the neutral alcohol.
Step 1 of 2

Yields: HCHO + RMgX → 1° Alcohol. RCHO + RMgX → 2° Alcohol. R₂CO + RMgX → 3° Alcohol.

⭐ Key Points

  • Hydroboration-Oxidation is ideal for anti-Markownikoff hydration.
  • Formaldehyde + Grignard = 1° alcohol; other aldehydes = 2°; ketones = 3°.
▶

Reactions of Alcohols

1. Cleavage of O-H Bond (Acidity)

  • Reaction with Na: 2R-OH + 2Na → 2R-O⁻Na⁺ + H₂↑. Shows weakly acidic nature.
  • Acidity Order: Water > 1° > 2° > 3° Alcohols. Electron-donating alkyl groups decrease acidity.
  • Esterification: R-OH + R'COOH ⇌ R'COOR + H₂O (Acid catalysed).

2. Cleavage of C-O Bond

  • Lucas Test (HCl/ZnCl₂): Distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols.
    • 3°: Turbidity appears immediately.
    • 2°: Turbidity in 5 minutes.
    • 1°: No turbidity at room temperature.

3. Dehydration of Alcohols (E1 Mechanism)

🔬 Reaction: Dehydration of Alcohols
Overall: R-CH₂-CH₂-OH (H⁺/Δ) → R-CH=CH₂ + H₂O
R-OH + H⁺ ⇌ R-OH₂⁺
Step 1: Protonation
The alcohol oxygen gets protonated by the acid.
Why? OH⁻ is a poor leaving group. Protonation converts it into H₂O, an excellent leaving group.
R-OH₂⁺ ⇌ R⁺ + H₂O
Step 2: Carbocation Formation
Water leaves, forming a carbocation. This is the slow step.
Why? C-O bond breaks. Stability order: 3° > 2° > 1°, hence 3° alcohols dehydrate easiest.
H-C-C⁺ → C=C + H⁺
Step 3: Deprotonation
A proton is lost to form the alkene. Follows Saytzeff Rule.
Why? Loss of H⁺ from adjacent carbon restores the electron pair and completes the double bond.
Step 1 of 3

4. Oxidation & Dehydrogenation

  • Oxidation:
    • 1° Alcohol → Aldehyde (PCC/CrO₃) → Acid (KMnO₄/K₂Cr₂O₇).
    • 2° Alcohol → Ketone (CrO₃).
    • 3° Alcohol → No reaction (Requires severe conditions, cleaves C-C bonds).
  • Dehydrogenation (Cu/573K): 1° gives aldehyde, 2° gives ketone, 3° gives alkene (dehydration!).

⭐ Key Points

  • Alcohols are weaker acids than water.
  • Lucas test differentiates based on carbocation stability.
  • Dehydration reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°.

Phenols

Preparation

  • Dow's Process: Chlorobenzene + NaOH (623K, 300atm) → Sodium phenoxide → Phenol.
  • From Cumene (Isopropylbenzene): Cumene is oxidized to cumene hydroperoxide, which is cleaved by acid into Phenol + Acetone (excellent yield of both).

Acidity of Phenols

Phenols are stronger acids than alcohols and water because the phenoxide ion is stabilized by resonance. Subsituent effects:

  • Electron Withdrawing Groups (-NO₂): Increase acidity (stabilize phenoxide). Greater effect at ortho/para.
  • Electron Donating Groups (-CH₃): Decrease acidity (destabilize phenoxide).

Chemical Reactions

Phenol undergoes Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution very easily due to activation by -OH ring.

🔬 Reaction: Kolbe's Reaction
Overall: Phenol + CO₂ / NaOH (H⁺) → Salicylic acid
Ph-OH + NaOH → Ph-O⁻Na⁺
Step 1: Formation of Phenoxide
Phenol reacts with NaOH to form sodium phenoxide.
Why? Phenoxide ion is even more reactive towards electrophilic substitution than phenol.
Ph-O⁻ + CO₂ → Ortho-attack intermediate
Step 2: Electrophilic Attack
Weak electrophile CO₂ attacks the ortho position.
Why? Ortho position is preferred due to proximity and chelation in the transition state.
Intermediate + H⁺ → Salicylic acid (2-Hydroxybenzoic acid)
Step 3: Acidification
Acid workup yields salicylic acid.
Why? Restores aromaticity and protonates the carboxylate.
Step 1 of 3
🔬 Reaction: Reimer-Tiemann Reaction
Overall: Phenol + CHCl₃ + aq. NaOH → Salicylaldehyde
CHCl₃ + OH⁻ → :CCl₂ + H₂O + Cl⁻
Step 1: Carbene Generation
Chloroform reacts with base to generate Dichlorocarbene.
Why? :CCl₂ is an electron-deficient electrophile.
Ph-O⁻ + :CCl₂ → ortho-CHCl₂ intermediate
Step 2: Electrophilic Attack
Dichlorocarbene attacks phenoxide at the ortho position.
Why? Highly activated ring facilitates attack by the carbene.
ortho-CHCl₂ + 2NaOH → ortho-CHO
Step 3: Hydrolysis
Alkaline hydrolysis of the -CHCl₂ group gives the aldehyde (Salicylaldehyde).
Why? Disubstitution of Cl with OH creates an unstable gem-diol which loses water to form -CHO.

  • Oxidation: Phenol + Na₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄ → Benzoquinone (diketone).

⭐ Key Points

  • Phenol is significantly more acidic than alcohols due to resonance stabilization of phenoxide.
  • Kolbe yields Salicylic acid (uses CO₂).
  • Reimer-Tiemann yields Salicylaldehyde (uses CHCl₃ + NaOH, via carbene).

Ethers

Preparation

🔬 Reaction: Williamson Ether Synthesis
Overall: R-X + R'-O⁻Na⁺ → R-O-R' + NaX
R'-O⁻ + R-X → [R'-O---R---X]⁻ → R'-O-R + X⁻
Step 1: SN2 Attack
The alkoxide ion attacks the alkyl halide via SN2 mechanism.
Why? The alkyl halide MUST be 1° or methyl. If a 2° or 3° halide is used, the strong alkoxide base causes E2 elimination instead, yielding alkenes! To make t-butyl methyl ether, use sodium t-butoxide + methyl bromide.
Step 1 of 1
🧬 NEET Tip

Always use 1° Alkyl Halide in Williamson synthesis. If you use a 3° halide (like t-butyl bromide), elimination dominates and you get an alkene instead of an ether!

Chemical Reactions

  • Cleavage by HI: R-O-R' + HI → R-OH + R'-I.
    • If mixed ether (1° & 2° groups), I⁻ attacks the smaller alkyl group via SN2 (steric reasons).
    • If one group is 3°, it follows SN1 mechanism, so I⁻ attacks the 3° group (carbocation stability).
    • With excess HI: R-O-R' + 2HI → 2RI + H₂O.
    • Alkyl aryl ethers: Cleaves to Phenol + Alkyl Iodide (Aryl-O bond is stronger due to resonance).
  • Electrophilic Substitution of Anisole: Alkoxy group is activating and ortho/para directing. Undergoes halogenation, nitration, Friedel-Crafts easily.

⭐ Key Points

  • Williamson synthesis requires 1° alkyl halides.
  • Ether cleavage with HI: follows SN2 (attacks smaller group) unless a 3° group is present (follows SN1, attacks 3° group).
  • Alkyl aryl ethers always yield phenol + alkyl halide upon HI cleavage.

Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids

Carbonyl compounds: The most important class of organic compounds for Board + NEET.

▶

Nomenclature & Structure

Carbonyl compounds contain the carbon-oxygen double bond (>C=O). In aldehydes, it is attached to at least one H atom; in ketones, to two carbon atoms; in carboxylic acids, to an -OH group.

IUPAC Naming Rules

  1. Aldehydes: Replace -e of alkane with -al. Numbering starts from the CHO carbon.
  2. Ketones: Replace -e with -one. Numbering gives the lowest locant to the C=O group.
  3. Carboxylic Acids: Replace -e with -oic acid.

Structure of Carbonyl Group

  • The carbonyl carbon is sp² hybridized.
  • The C=O bond is highly polar due to oxygen's electronegativity (C is δ+, O is δ-).
  • This polarity makes the carbon an electrophilic center, susceptible to nucleophilic-addition.
📝 Reactivity Comparison

Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition because:

  • Steric effect: Two bulky alkyl groups in ketones hinder nucleophilic attack.
  • Electronic effect: Two electron-donating alkyl groups reduce the +ve charge (δ+) on the carbonyl carbon via +I effect.

Acyl chloride is reduced to aldehyde over palladium catalyst supported on barium sulfate.
Why BaSO₄? It acts as a catalytic poison to prevent further reduction of the aldehyde to a primary alcohol.

Preparation of Ketones

  • From Alcohols: Oxidation of 2° alcohols (CrO₃, KMnO₄, or heated Cu).
  • From Acyl Chlorides: Reaction with dialkylcadmium (R₂Cd).
    2 R'-COCl + R₂Cd → 2 R'-CO-R + CdCl₂
  • From Nitriles: Reaction with grignard reagent followed by hydrolysis.

Preparation of Carboxylic Acids

  • From 1° Alcohols & Aldehydes: Strong oxidation (KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇).
  • From Nitriles & Amides: Complete hydrolysis with H⁺ or OH⁻.
  • From Grignard Reagents: Reaction with solid CO₂ (dry ice) followed by hydrolysis.

  • Ketones are specifically prepared using R₂Cd to avoid forming 3° alcohols.
  • Grignard + CO₂ is a standard way to increase carbon chain by 1 to make an acid.

Nucleophilic Addition Reactions

The characteristic reaction of aldehydes and ketones is nucleophilic addition to the carbonyl group.

🔬 Reaction: Nucleophilic Addition Mechanism
Overall: >C=O + Nu⁻ + H⁺ → >C(OH)Nu
Nu⁻ approaches planar sp² Carbon (δ+)
Step 1: Nucleophilic Attack
The nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon perpendicularly to the plane. The π bond breaks, pushing electrons onto oxygen.
Why? The carbon is electron-deficient due to the electronegative oxygen.
Intermediate: Tetrahedral Alkoxide Ion
Step 2: Tetrahedral Intermediate
The hybridization of carbon changes from sp² to sp³, forming a tetrahedral alkoxide ion intermediate.
Why? Four sigma bonds are formed, requiring tetrahedral geometry.
Alkoxide + H⁺ → Addition Product
Step 3: Protonation
The negatively charged oxygen captures a proton from the reaction medium to form the neutral addition product.
Why? Acid-base rapid proton transfer stabilizes the negative charge.

Key Addition Reactions

  • Addition of HCN: Yields cyanohydrins. Base catalyzed (creates CN⁻). (See viewer below).
  • Addition of NaHSO₃: Forms bisulfite addition compound (crystalline, used for purification).
  • Addition of Grignard: Formaldehyde → 1° alcohol; Other Aldehydes → 2° alcohol; Ketones → 3° alcohol.
  • Addition of Alcohols: Aldehydes + 1 eq alcohol → hemiacetal. + 2 eq → acetal. Ketones → ketals (using ethylene glycol).

Why? HCN is a weak acid; without base, CN⁻ concentration is too low for a fast reaction.
>C=O + CN⁻ → >C(O⁻)(CN)

CN⁻ attacks carbonyl carbon.
Why? CN⁻ is a strong nucleophile.
>C(O⁻)(CN) + H₂O → >C(OH)(CN) + OH⁻

Why? To form the stable cyanohydrin and regenerate the base catalyst.

Enolate attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second un-ionized aldehyde molecule.
Why? Enolate is a strong nucleophile, and the second molecule's C=O carbon is an electrophile.

Aldol + Heat → CH₃CH=CHCHO + H₂O

Heating causes loss of water to form α,β-unsaturated aldehyde.
Why? The resulting double bond is conjugated with the carbonyl, highly stabilizing the product.
🔬 Reaction: Cannizzaro Reaction
Overall: 2 HCHO + conc. KOH → HCOOK + CH₃OH

Hydroxide ion attacks the carbonyl carbon of one molecule lacking α-hydrogens.

HCH(OH)O⁻ + HCHO → HCOOH + CH₃O⁻
Step 2: Hydride Shift
A hydride ion (H⁻) transfers to the carbonyl carbon of the second molecule. One molecule is oxidized (to acid), one is reduced (to alkoxide).
Why? Reformation of the C=O double bond expels the hydride, which is a strong reducing agent.
HCOOH + CH₃O⁻ → HCOO⁻ + CH₃OH
Step 3: Acid-Base Exchange
Proton transfers from the newly formed acid to the strongly basic alkoxide ion.
Why? This forms the highly stable carboxylate ion.

Carbonyls with a methyl group attached to the C=O carbon (CH₃-CO-) react with NaOX (NaOH + X₂) to give haloform (CHX₃) and carboxylate salt.

  • Cannizzaro: No α-H. Conc. base. Disproportionation (Oxidation + Reduction).

Carboxylic Acids

Physical Properties

  • Boiling Points: Higher than alcohols of comparable mass due to formation of cyclic dimers via extensive H-bonding.
  • Solubility: Lower members miscible in water; solubility decreases as hydrophobic alkyl chain increases.

Acidity of Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids are acidic because the carboxylate anion (RCOO⁻) is resonance stabilized.

📝 Substituent Effects

Electron Withdrawing Groups (EWG): Stabilize carboxylate ion via -I effect, INCREASING acidity.
Electron Donating Groups (EDG): Destabilize carboxylate ion via +I effect, DECREASING acidity.

Acidity Order: Cl₃CCOOH > Cl₂CHCOOH > ClCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH > HCOOH

Wait, anomaly! Actually, HCOOH is stronger than CH₃COOH. Correct order: Cl₃CCOOH > Cl₂CHCOOH > ClCH₂COOH > HCOOH > CH₃COOH. This is because CH₃ has a +I effect which destabilizes the anion relative to H.

Important Reactions

🔬 Reaction: Fischer Esterification
Overall: R-COOH + R'-OH ⇌ R-COOR' + H₂O (Acid catalyzed)
R-COOH + H⁺ ⇌ Protonated Acid
Step 1: Protonation
Carbonyl oxygen is protonated.
Why? Increases electrophilicity of carbonyl carbon.
Protonated Acid + R'-OH ⇌ Tetrahedral Intermediate
Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack
Alcohol attacks the carbonyl carbon.
Why? The alcohol oxygen has lone pairs.
Proton Transfer → Loss of H₂O → Ester
Step 3: Elimination of Water
Proton transfers to OH group, making it a good leaving group (H₂O). Water leaves, forming ester.
Why? Formation of the stable C=O double bond drives off the leaving group.
  • Reduction: LiAlH₄ reduces acids directly to 1° alcohols. (NaBH₄ does not).
  • Decarboxylation: Sodium salt heated with soda lime (NaOH + CaO, 3:1) gives alkane with one less carbon.

Acid converts to acyl halide.
Why? Acyl halides undergo enolization much faster than free acids.
R-CH₂-COX ⇌ R-CH=C(OH)X + X₂ → R-CH(X)-COX
Step 2: α-Halogenation
Enol reacts with halogen at the α-position.
Why? The enol double bond is nucleophilic.
R-CH(X)-COX + H₂O → R-CH(X)-COOH + HX
Step 3: Hydrolysis
Water hydrolyzes the α-haloacyl halide back to the acid.
Why? Acyl halides are readily hydrolyzed.

⭐ Key Points

  • Acids exist as dimers, giving them unusually high boiling points.
  • EWG increases acidity (-I effect); EDG decreases acidity (+I effect). HCOOH > CH₃COOH.
  • HVZ reaction specifically halogenates the α-carbon of carboxylic acids.
Chapter 6

Amines

Derivatives of ammonia, crucial for organic synthesis and biological systems.

▶

Classification & Nomenclature

  • 1° (Primary) Amine: R-NH₂ (One alkyl group attached to N)
  • 2° (Secondary) Amine: R-NH-R' (Two alkyl groups attached to N)
  • 3° (Tertiary) Amine: R₃N (Three alkyl groups attached to N)

Structure: The nitrogen atom in amines is sp³ hybridized with pyramidal geometry (bond angle ~108°) due to the presence of one lone pair.

Nomenclature: Aliphatic amines are named as alkanamines. In 2° and 3° amines, smaller groups are named as N-alkyl substituents (e.g., N-methylethanamine).

⭐ Key Points

  • Classification is based on the number of alkyl/aryl groups attached DIRECTLY to Nitrogen (unlike alcohols where it's based on the Carbon).
  • Geometry is pyramidal due to sp³ hybridization and lone pair repulsion.
▶

Preparation of Amines

  • Ammonolysis of Alkyl Halides: NH₃ + R-X → R-NH₂.
    Problem: Yields a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3°, and quaternary ammonium salts. (Over-alkylation).
  • Reduction of Nitro Compounds: Ar-NO₂ reduced to Ar-NH₂ using Sn/HCl or H₂/Pd.
  • Reduction of Nitriles/Amides: Using LiAlH₄.
🔬 Reaction: Gabriel Phthalimide Synthesis
Overall: Phthalimide → 1° Aliphatic Amine
Phthalimide + KOH → Potassium Phthalimide
Step 1: Salt Formation
KOH abstracts the acidic proton from phthalimide to form a nucleophilic salt.
Why? The N-H proton is acidic because the conjugate base is stabilized by two carbonyl groups.
K-Phthalimide + R-X → N-Alkylphthalimide
Step 2: SN2 Attack
The phthalimide anion acts as a nucleophile, displacing the halide from R-X.
Why? This forms a C-N bond without risk of over-alkylation (steric bulk prevents multiple substitutions).
N-Alkylphthalimide + NaOH (aq) → 1° Amine + Phthalate salt
Step 3: Hydrolysis
Alkaline hydrolysis releases the pure primary aliphatic amine.
Why? Cleaves the amide bonds. Aromatic primary amines cannot be prepared this way as aryl halides do not undergo SN2 easily.
🔬 Reaction: Hofmann Bromamide Degradation
Overall: R-CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4 NaOH → R-NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2 NaBr + 2 H₂O
R-CONH₂ + Br₂/NaOH → R-CONHBr
Step 1: N-Bromination
Amide reacts with Br₂ in base to form N-bromoamide.
Why? The amide nitrogen is nucleophilic enough to attack bromine.
R-CONHBr + Base → Formation of Nitrene intermediate & Rearrangement
Step 2: Migration
The alkyl group migrates from carbonyl carbon to nitrogen, forming an isocyanate (R-N=C=O).
Why? Loss of Br⁻ and migration occur concertedly to stabilize the electron-deficient nitrogen.
R-N=C=O + 2 NaOH → R-NH₂ + Na₂CO₃
Step 3: Hydrolysis
Isocyanate is rapidly hydrolyzed by the aqueous base to form the 1° amine.
Why? The amine formed has one carbon LESS than the starting amide (degradation).

⭐ Key Points

  • Gabriel Synthesis gives PURE 1° aliphatic amines (no over-alkylation). Cannot make aniline.
  • Hofmann degradation reduces the carbon chain length by one (step-down reaction).
▶

Chemical Properties

Basicity of Amines

Amines are basic due to the lone pair on nitrogen. (basicity). Aliphatic amines are stronger bases than NH₃ due to +I effect of alkyl groups. Aromatic amines (aniline) are much weaker because the lone pair is involved in resonance with the ring.

📝 Basicity Order in Aqueous Solution

Basicity depends on +I effect, steric hindrance, and solvation (H-bonding with water).

  • Methyl amines: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃ (i.e., (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N)
  • Ethyl amines: 2° > 3° > 1° > NH₃ (i.e., (C₂H₅)₂NH > (C₂H₅)₃N > C₂H₅NH₂)
🔬 Reaction: Carbylamine Reaction (Isocyanide Test)
Overall: R-NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3 KOH → R-NC + 3 KCl + 3 H₂O
Reagents mixed and heated
Step 1: Reaction
Aliphatic or aromatic 1° amines react with chloroform and alc. KOH.
Why? Dichlorocarbene (:CCl₂) is formed as an intermediate which attacks the amine.
Foul smelling gas produced
Step 2: Observation
An intensely foul-smelling substance, an isocyanide (carbylamine), is formed.
Why? This is a specific qualitative test ONLY for 1° amines. 2° and 3° do not react.

Reaction with Nitrous Acid (HNO₂)

  • 1° Aliphatic: Forms unstable diazonium salt, decomposes to Alcohol + N₂ gas (effervescence).
  • 1° Aromatic: Forms stable diazonium salt (ArN₂⁺X⁻) at 0-5°C.
  • 2° Amines: Form N-nitrosamines (yellow oily liquid).

Hinsberg Test

Uses benzenesulfonyl chloride (Hinsberg reagent) to distinguish 1°, 2°, 3° amines.

AmineReaction with ReagentSolubility in Alkali
1° AmineForms N-alkylbenzenesulfonamideSoluble (due to acidic N-H)
2° AmineForms N,N-dialkylbenzenesulfonamideInsoluble (no acidic H on N)
3° AmineNo reactionInsoluble (amine itself forms layers)

Electrophilic Substitution of Aniline

-NH₂ is highly activating (o,p-directing). Bromination with Br₂(aq) directly gives 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (white ppt). To get mono-substitution, the -NH₂ group must be protected by acetylation (forming acetanilide) before reaction, then hydrolyzed.

⭐ Key Points

  • Aniline is a weaker base than ammonia.
  • Carbylamine test: Specific for 1° amines (foul smell).
  • Hinsberg test differentiates 1°, 2°, 3° amines.
  • Protection of -NH₂ is required to prevent polysubstitution in aniline.

Diazonium Salts

Diazonium salts (ArN₂⁺X⁻) are prepared by Diazotization of aniline with NaNO₂ + HCl at 0-5 °C. They are incredibly useful for synthesizing substituted benzenes.

🔬 Reaction: Sandmeyer Reaction
Overall: ArN₂⁺X⁻ + CuCl/HCl → Ar-Cl + N₂
ArN₂⁺ + Cu(I) salt
Step 1: Electron Transfer
Cu(I) catalyzes the decomposition of the diazonium ion, likely via a radical mechanism.
Why? The N₂ group is one of the best leaving-groups in chemistry, making substitution very favorable.
Ar-Radical + Ligand → Ar-X
Step 2: Substitution
The halide or cyanide from the copper salt attaches to the aromatic ring. (Yields Ar-Cl, Ar-Br, Ar-CN).
Why? Offers a direct route to halobenzenes and benzonitriles which are hard to make directly.
  • Gattermann: Similar to Sandmeyer but uses Cu powder + HX instead of Cu(I) salts.
  • Balz-Schiemann: ArN₂⁺ + HBF₄ → ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ (ppt). Heating yields Ar-F (Fluorobenzene) + BF₃ + N₂.
  • Replacement by H: Use H₃PO₂ (hypophosphorous acid) or Ethanol (reduces ArN₂⁺ to Benzene).
🔬 Reaction: Coupling Reaction
Overall: ArN₂⁺ + Ar'-OH → Ar-N=N-Ar'-OH (Azo Dye)
Diazonium Ion + Phenol (in mildly basic medium)
Step 1: Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution
The diazonium ion acts as a weak electrophile and attacks the electron-rich para-position of phenol.
Why? Phenol is highly activated by the -OH group, allowing reaction with weak electrophiles. Basic medium creates the even more reactive phenoxide ion.
Formation of p-Hydroxyazobenzene
Step 2: Azo Dye Formation
The product contains an extended conjugated system (-N=N- linkage), giving it intense color (orange dye).
Why? Conjugation lowers the energy gap for electron excitation, moving absorption into the visible light spectrum. (Aniline couples in mildly acidic medium to give yellow dye).

⭐ Key Points

  • Diazonium salts must be kept cold (0-5 °C) to prevent decomposition into phenol.
  • Sandmeyer (Cu salts) is generally better yielding than Gattermann (Cu powder).
  • Coupling reactions create brightly colored azo dyes (Phenol = Orange, Aniline = Yellow).

Biomolecules

Structure, properties, and functions of carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids, and vitamins.

▶

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds which produce such units on hydrolysis.

Classification based on Hydrolysis

  1. Monosaccharides: Simplest carbohydrates, cannot be hydrolysed further (e.g., Glucose, Fructose, Ribose).
  2. Oligosaccharides: Yield 2-10 monosaccharide units on hydrolysis (e.g., Sucrose, Maltose, Lactose are disaccharides).
  3. Polysaccharides: Yield a large number of monosaccharide units (e.g., Starch, Cellulose, Glycogen).
⭐ NEET Tip

Reducing sugars: Have free aldehyde or ketone groups (reduce Fehling's/Tollens' reagent). All monosaccharides, maltose, lactose.
Non-reducing sugars: Do not have free aldehyde/ketone groups. Sucrose.

Monosaccharides

Glucose (Aldohexose): Structure elucidated by specific reactions:

  • HI/Δ: Forms n-hexane (straight chain of 6 carbons).
  • NH₂OH: Forms oxime (presence of carbonyl group).
  • Br₂ water: Oxidizes to gluconic acid (aldehyde group present).
  • Acetic anhydride: Forms pentaacetate (presence of 5 -OH groups).

Cyclic Structure: Exists in two cyclic hemiacetal forms (α and β anomers) represented by Haworth structures.

Fructose (Ketohexose): Has a ketone functional group at C-2. Forms a 5-membered furanose ring in cyclic form.

Disaccharides (Joined by glycosidic linkage)

  • Sucrose: α-D-glucose and β-D-fructose (C1-C2 linkage). Non-reducing sugar.
  • Maltose: Two α-D-glucose units (C1-C4 linkage). Reducing sugar.
  • Lactose: β-D-galactose and β-D-glucose (C1-C4 linkage). Reducing sugar.

Polysaccharides

  • Starch: Plant storage polymer of α-glucose. Two components: Amylose (water-soluble, linear, C1-C4) and Amylopectin (water-insoluble, branched, C1-C4 and C1-C6).
  • Cellulose: Plant cell wall component. Linear polymer of β-D-glucose (C1-C4 linkage).
  • Glycogen: Animal starch. Highly branched polymer of α-glucose.

⭐ Key Points

  • Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes/ketones.
  • Glucose open chain confirmed by HI, NH₂OH, Br₂ water.
  • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, while Maltose and Lactose are reducing.
  • Starch = Amylose + Amylopectin; Cellulose = β-glucose polymer.

Proteins and Enzymes

Proteins are polymers of α-amino acids connected by peptide bonds.

Amino Acids

  • Contain amino (-NH₂) and carboxyl (-COOH) functional groups.
  • Zwitterion: At a specific pH (isoelectric point), the -COOH loses a proton and -NH₂ gains a proton, forming a neutral dipolar ion.
  • Essential amino acids: Cannot be synthesized in the body (e.g., Valine, Leucine).
  • Non-essential amino acids: Synthesized in the body (e.g., Glycine, Alanine).
🔬 Reaction: Peptide Bond Formation
Amino Acid 1 + Amino Acid 2 → Dipeptide + H₂O
H₂N-CH(R₁)-COOH + H₂N-CH(R₂)-COOH
Step 1: Alignment of molecules
The carboxyl group of one amino acid aligns with the amino group of another.
Why? This orientation allows for a condensation reaction between the acid and amine functional groups.
H₂N-CH(R₁)-CO-NH-CH(R₂)-COOH + H₂O
Step 2: Condensation and Elimination of Water
The -OH from the carboxyl group and -H from the amino group are removed as water (H₂O), forming an amide (-CO-NH-) bond.
Why? This is an acid-catalyzed condensation forming the strong covalent peptide bond linking amino acids.
Step 1 of 2

Structure of Proteins

  1. Primary (1°): Specific sequence of amino acids.
  2. Secondary (2°): Folding of the polypeptide chain into α-helix or β-pleated sheet via hydrogen bonding.
  3. Tertiary (3°): Overall 3D folding due to H-bonds, disulfide linkages, van der Waals, and electrostatic forces.
  4. Quaternary (4°): Spatial arrangement of two or more polypeptide subunits.
⭐ NEET Tip

The primary structure dictates all higher-order structures. Even a single amino acid change (like in sickle cell anemia) can drastically alter protein function.

Classification & Denaturation

  • Fibrous Proteins: Polypeptide chains run parallel, held by H-bonds/disulfide bonds. Insoluble in water (e.g., Keratin, Myosin).
  • Globular Proteins: Chains fold into a spherical shape. Soluble in water (e.g., Insulin, Albumin).
  • Denaturation: Changes in pH or temperature disrupt 2° and 3° structures (1° structure remains intact), causing loss of biological activity (e.g., boiling an egg).

Enzymes

Biocatalysts (mostly globular proteins) that are highly specific. They operate via the lock-and-key model where the substrate fits precisely into the active site.

⭐ Key Points

  • Amino acids exist as zwitterions at their isoelectric point.
  • Proteins are linked by peptide (-CO-NH-) bonds.
  • Protein structures range from primary (sequence) to quaternary (multiple subunits).
  • Denaturation destroys 2° and 3° structure, keeping 1° intact.

Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are biopolymers of nucleotides responsible for the transmission of genetic information and protein synthesis.

Components of Nucleic Acids

  • Pentose Sugar: β-D-ribose in RNA, β-D-2-deoxyribose in DNA.
  • Phosphoric Acid Group: Links sugars via phosphodiester linkage.
  • Nitrogenous Bases:
    • Purines: Adenine (A), Guanine (G).
    • Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T, only DNA), Uracil (U, only RNA).
ℹ️ Nucleoside vs Nucleotide

Nucleoside = Sugar + Base (attached at 1' position).
Nucleotide = Sugar + Base + Phosphate (attached at 5' position of sugar).

DNA Structure

James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double helix model. Two polynucleotide chains run anti-parallel and are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs:

  • Adenine pairs with Thymine (A=T, 2 H-bonds)
  • Guanine pairs with Cytosine (G≡C, 3 H-bonds)
⭐ NEET Tip

Chargaff's Rule: In double-stranded DNA, the amount of Purines equals the amount of Pyrimidines. (A + G = C + T).

Types of RNA and Central Dogma

  • mRNA (messenger): Carries genetic code from DNA to ribosomes.
  • rRNA (ribosomal): Structural component of ribosomes.
  • tRNA (transfer): Brings amino acids to the ribosome during protein synthesis.

Central Dogma: DNA → (Transcription) → RNA → (Translation) → Protein

Nucleic Acid Replication

DNA replication is semi-conservative; the two strands separate and each acts as a template for synthesizing a new complementary strand.

Feature DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) RNA (Ribonucleic Acid)
Sugar β-D-2-deoxyribose β-D-ribose
Pyrimidines Cytosine (C) and Thymine (T) Cytosine (C) and Uracil (U)
Structure Double-stranded helix Single-stranded (usually)
Function Stores genetic information Protein synthesis

⭐ Key Points

  • Nucleotide = Sugar + Base + Phosphate.
  • DNA contains A, G, C, T; RNA contains A, G, C, U.
  • DNA is a double helix with A=T and G≡C base pairing.
  • Central Dogma describes information flow from DNA to Protein.

Vitamins and Hormones

Vitamins are essential organic compounds required in small amounts in our diet for normal growth, maintenance, and biological functions.

Classification of Vitamins

  • Fat-Soluble: Vitamins A, D, E, K. Stored in liver and adipose tissues.
  • Water-Soluble: Vitamins B-complex and C. Must be supplied regularly as they are excreted in urine (except B₁₂).

Hormones (Brief Overview)

Hormones are intercellular messengers produced by endocrine glands and poured directly into the bloodstream to regulate biological processes. Examples include Insulin (regulates blood sugar), Thyroxine (metabolism), and Epinephrine.

  • Deficiency of Vitamin C causes Scurvy, Vitamin A causes Night blindness.
  • Hormones act as chemical messengers from endocrine glands.
