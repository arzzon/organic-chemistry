Some Basic Principles of Organic Chemistry

Classification, nomenclature, isomerism, and basic mechanisms underlying organic reactions.

▶

Classification of Organic Compounds

Organic compounds are broadly classified based on their structural framework.

1. Based on Structure

  • Open-chain (Acyclic) or Aliphatic: Straight or branched chains of carbon atoms. E.g., Butane, Isobutane.
  • Closed-chain (Cyclic) or Ring Compounds:
    • Homocyclic (Carbocyclic): Ring contains only carbon atoms. E.g., Cyclohexane, Benzene (aromatic).
    • Heterocyclic: Ring contains carbon and at least one other heteroatom (O, N, S). E.g., Pyridine, Furan.

2. Functional Groups Table

A functional group is an atom or group of atoms joined in a specific manner which determines the chemical properties of the organic compound.

3. Homologous Series

A series of organic compounds with the same functional group and similar chemical properties in which successive members differ by a -CH₂- group (14u mass).

🧬 NEET Tip
Physical properties (like boiling point) generally increase with molecular mass in a homologous series due to increased van der Waals forces, but chemical properties remain very similar.

⭐ Key Points

  • Organic compounds are classified into open-chain and closed-chain.
  • Functional groups dictate the chemical behavior of the molecule.
  • Homologous series differ by a CH₂ group.

IUPAC Nomenclature

A systematic method of naming organic chemical compounds as recommended by IUPAC.

Rules for Naming:

  1. Longest Chain Rule: Select the longest continuous carbon chain containing the principal functional group, multiple bonds, or maximum substituents.
  2. Numbering Rule: Number the chain to give the lowest possible locants to the principal functional group, then double/triple bonds, then substituents.
  3. Alphabetical Order: Prefix names of substituents are listed alphabetically.
  4. Format: Prefix(es) + Word root + Primary suffix (ane/ene/yne) + Secondary suffix (functional group).

Naming Examples

  • Alcohol: CH₃-CH(OH)-CH₃ is Propan-2-ol.
  • Aldehyde: CH₃-CH₂-CHO is Propanal.
  • Ketone: CH₃-CO-CH₂-CH₃ is Butan-2-one.
  • Carboxylic Acid: CH₃-CH(CH₃)-COOH is 2-Methylpropanoic acid.
  • Amine: CH₃-CH₂-NH₂ is Ethanamine.
  • Halide: CH₃-CHCl-CH₃ is 2-Chloropropane.
  • Ether: CH₃-O-CH₂-CH₃ is Methoxyethane.
⚠️ Common Mistake
When naming compounds with both double and triple bonds, double bonds get priority in numbering if both get the same locant from either end (e.g., But-1-en-3-yne, not But-3-en-1-yne).

⭐ Key Points

  • Find the longest chain containing the principal group.
  • Number to minimize locants.
  • Assemble using: prefix + root + primary suffix + secondary suffix.

Isomerism

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulas or spatial arrangements.

1. Structural Isomerism

Compounds with different bonding patterns.

  • Chain: Different carbon skeleton (e.g., n-butane and isobutane).
  • Position: Different position of functional group or multiple bond (e.g., butan-1-ol and butan-2-ol).
  • Functional Group: Different functional groups (e.g., ethanol and dimethyl ether).
  • Metamerism: Different alkyl chains on either side of a polyvalent functional group (e.g., diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether).
  • Tautomerism: Dynamic equilibrium between two isomers, usually involving a proton shift (e.g., keto-enol tautomerism).

2. Stereoisomerism

Compounds with same bonding but different spatial arrangement.

Geometrical Isomerism (ref)

Arises due to restricted rotation around a double bond or in a ring.

  • Cis: Similar groups on the same side.
  • Trans: Similar groups on opposite sides.
  • E/Z System: Used when 3 or 4 different groups are attached. Assign priorities using CIP rules. Z = high priority groups on same side (Zusammen), E = opposite (Entgegen).
Optical Isomerism (ref)

Compounds that rotate the plane of polarized light.

  • Chirality (ref): A molecule is chiral if it is non-superimposable on its mirror image (usually has an asymmetric carbon/chiral centre).
  • Enantiomers: Non-superimposable mirror images. Rotate light by equal amounts in opposite directions.
  • Diastereomers: Stereoisomers that are not mirror images of each other.
  • Meso Compounds: Have chiral centres but are optically inactive due to an internal plane of symmetry.
R/S Configuration (CIP Rules)
  1. Assign priorities to the 4 groups on the chiral centre based on atomic number.
  2. View the molecule with the lowest priority group pointing away.
  3. If the sequence 1 → 2 → 3 is clockwise, it is R (Rectus). If anti-clockwise, it is S (Sinister).

⭐ Key Points

  • Structural isomers differ in bonding (chain, position, functional, metamerism, tautomerism).
  • Stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement (geometrical and optical).
  • Chirality implies non-superimposable mirror images; CIP rules assign E/Z and R/S.

Electronic Effects

Electronic effects permanently or temporarily alter the electron distribution in organic molecules, impacting stability and reactivity.

1. Inductive Effect (ref)

Permanent polarization of a σ bond due to differences in electronegativity. It operates through σ bonds and decreases rapidly with distance.

  • -I Effect (Electron Withdrawing): Draws electrons away. E.g., -NO₂ > -CN > -F > -Cl > -Br > -I > -OH > -NH₂
  • +I Effect (Electron Donating): Pushes electrons. E.g., -C(CH₃)₃ > -CH(CH₃)₂ > -CH₂CH₃ > -CH₃

2. Resonance / Mesomeric Effect (ref)

Delocalization of π electrons or lone pairs. Permanent effect.

  • +M / +R Effect: Donates electrons to the conjugated system via lone pair or π bond. E.g., -OH, -OR, -NH₂, -Cl.
  • -M / -R Effect: Withdraws electrons from the conjugated system. E.g., -NO₂, -CN, -CHO, -COOH.

3. Hyperconjugation (ref)

Delocalization of σ electrons of an α-C-H bond into an adjacent empty or partially filled p-orbital or π-orbital. Also called "no-bond resonance".

More α-hydrogens = more hyperconjugative structures = greater stability.

4. Electromeric Effect

Temporary, complete transfer of π electrons to one of the bonded atoms in the presence of an attacking reagent.

  • +E: Transfer to the atom where reagent attacks.
  • -E: Transfer away from the atom where reagent attacks.

Stability of Intermediates

IntermediateStabilized ByOrder of Stability
Carbocation (C⁺)+I, +M, Hyperconjugation3° > 2° > 1° > Methyl⁺
Free Radical (C•)+I, +M, Hyperconjugation3° > 2° > 1° > Methyl•
Carbanion (C⁻)-I, -MMethyl⁻ > 1° > 2° > 3°
📝 Note
Resonance generally dominates over hyperconjugation, which dominates over the inductive effect (R > H > I), except for halogens where -I > +M.

⭐ Key Points

  • Inductive effect acts via σ bonds; depends on electronegativity.
  • Resonance/Mesomeric effect involves π/lone pair delocalization.
  • Hyperconjugation involves σ-C-H bond delocalization.
  • Stability orders: Carbocation (3°>2°>1°), Radical (3°>2°>1°), Carbanion (1°>2°>3°).

Reaction Mechanisms

A reaction mechanism is the step-by-step description of bond breaking and bond making in a chemical reaction.

1. Bond Fission

  • Homolysis (ref): Symmetrical breaking of a covalent bond. Each atom takes one electron. Forms free radicals. Promoted by HEAT, LIGHT, or PEROXIDES.
  • Heterolysis (ref): Unsymmetrical breaking. More electronegative atom takes both electrons. Forms carbocations and carbanions.

2. Types of Reagents

  • Nucleophiles (Nu⁻): "Nucleus loving". Electron-rich species that attack electron-deficient centers. E.g., OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃, H₂O.
  • Electrophiles (E⁺): "Electron loving". Electron-deficient species that attack electron-rich centers. E.g., H⁺, NO₂⁺, AlCl₃, BF₃.

3. Types of Organic Reactions

  • Substitution: Replacement of an atom/group by another. Can be Nucleophilic (SN1, SN2), Electrophilic (EAS), or Free Radical.
  • Addition: Reagent adds to a multiple bond (no byproducts). Usually Electrophilic (alkenes/alkynes) or Nucleophilic (carbonyls).
  • Elimination: Removal of atoms/groups to form a multiple bond (usually forms byproducts like H₂O or HX).
  • Rearrangement: Migration of an atom/group within a molecule to form a more stable isomer (e.g., carbocation rearrangement).

4. Arrow Notation

  • Curved Arrow: Shows movement of an electron pair. Starts at a lone pair or bond, ends at the atom receiving the pair.
  • Fish-hook Arrow (Half-headed): Shows movement of a single electron (in homolysis).

⭐ Key Points

  • Homolysis yields free radicals; heterolysis yields ions.
  • Nucleophiles are electron-rich; Electrophiles are electron-deficient.
  • Major reaction types: Substitution, Addition, Elimination, Rearrangement.

Hydrocarbons

Alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, and aromatic compounds: preparation, properties, and reactions.

▶

Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated open-chain hydrocarbons containing only carbon-carbon single bonds. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.

Preparation

  • Decarboxylation: Sodium salts of carboxylic acids heated with soda lime (NaOH + CaO) yield alkanes containing one carbon less.
    R-COONa + NaOH → CaO, Δ R-H + Na₂CO₃
  • Hydrogenation: Addition of H₂ to alkenes/alkynes in presence of finely divided Ni, Pd, or Pt.
🔬 Reaction: Wurtz Reaction
Overall: 2 R-X + 2 Na → R-R + 2 NaX
R-X + 2 Na (dry ether)
Step 1: Reaction of Alkyl Halide with Sodium
Alkyl halides react with sodium metal in dry ether to form higher symmetrical alkanes.
Why? The active metal Na donates electrons to form a radical or carbanion intermediate, coupling the alkyl groups. Best for forming symmetrical alkanes with an even number of carbon atoms.
Step 1 of 1

Chemical Reactions

Alkanes are generally inert but undergo substitution reactions under special conditions.

🔬 Reaction: Free Radical Halogenation
Overall: CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (in presence of UV light / hν)
Cl-Cl → hν 2 Cl•
Step 1: Initiation
UV light causes homolytic cleavage of the Cl-Cl bond.
Why? The Cl-Cl bond is relatively weak and can be cleaved by photons of appropriate energy, creating highly reactive chlorine free radicals.
Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃•
CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•
Step 2: Propagation
The chlorine radical abstracts a hydrogen from methane, creating a methyl radical. This methyl radical then attacks a Cl₂ molecule, creating the product and another Cl•.
Why? This is a chain reaction. Radicals are unstable and seek to pair their electrons. The newly formed radical perpetuates the cycle.
Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂
CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆
CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl
Step 3: Termination
Any two radicals combine to form a stable molecule.
Why? The chain reaction stops when radicals consume each other, terminating the chain propagation.
Step 1 of 3
🧬 NEET Tip
In halogenation, reactivity order of halogens is F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. Reactivity order of hydrogens is 3° > 2° > 1°. Selectivity for bromination is much higher than for chlorination.

⭐ Key Points

  • Wurtz reaction doubles the alkyl chain (best for symmetrical alkanes).
  • Free radical substitution (halogenation) follows initiation, propagation, termination.
▶

Alkenes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon double bond. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ.

Preparation

  • Dehydration of Alcohols: Heating alcohols with conc. H₂SO₄. Follows Saytzeff Rule: Highly substituted alkene is major product.
  • Dehydrohalogenation of Alkyl Halides: Heating with alcoholic KOH (beta-elimination). Also follows Saytzeff rule.

Addition Reactions

🔬 Reaction: Addition of HX (Markownikoff's Rule)
Overall: Propene + HBr → 2-Bromopropane
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃-CH⁺-CH₃ (2° carbocation)
Step 1: Electrophilic Attack by H⁺
The π electrons of the alkene attack the H⁺ from HBr. This forms a more stable secondary carbocation.
Why? Markownikoff's rule states the negative part goes to the carbon with fewer hydrogens. Mechanistically, this is because the reaction proceeds via the most stable intermediate (2° carbocation is more stable than 1° due to hyperconjugation).
CH₃-CH⁺-CH₃ + Br⁻ → CH₃-CHBr-CH₃
Step 2: Nucleophilic Attack by Br⁻
The bromide ion (Br⁻) attacks the positive carbon of the intermediate carbocation to form the final product.
Why? The nucleophile completes the addition by forming a bond with the electron-deficient carbocation.
Step 1 of 2
🔬 Reaction: Peroxide Effect (Anti-Markownikoff)
Overall: Propene + HBr (with Peroxides) → 1-Bromopropane
R-O-O-R → 2 R-O•
R-O• + HBr → R-OH + Br•
Step 1: Generation of Bromine Radical
Peroxides undergo homolytic cleavage. The resulting alkoxy radical abstracts a hydrogen from HBr, creating a bromine free radical.
Why? Peroxides are unstable and act as radical initiators. Note: This effect is ONLY observed with HBr, not HCl or HI.
CH₃-CH=CH₂ + Br• → CH₃-C•H-CH₂Br (2° radical)
Step 2: Addition of Br Radical
The Br• adds to the terminal carbon to form the more stable secondary free radical.
Why? Anti-Markownikoff addition. The reaction proceeds via the most stable free radical intermediate (2° radical > 1° radical).
CH₃-C•H-CH₂Br + HBr → CH₃-CH₂-CH₂Br + Br•
Step 3: Abstraction of Hydrogen
The secondary radical abstracts a hydrogen atom from another HBr molecule, completing the product and regenerating a Br• to continue the chain.
Why? This completes the chain propagation cycle, yielding the 1-bromo product.
Step 1 of 3

Ozonolysis: Alkene + O₃ followed by Zn/H₂O cleaves the double bond to form carbonyl compounds (aldehydes/ketones).

⭐ Key Points

  • Electrophilic addition of HX follows Markownikoff's rule (via carbocation).
  • Peroxide effect applies ONLY to HBr, resulting in anti-Markownikoff addition (via free radical).
  • Ozonolysis is a powerful tool to determine the position of a double bond.
▶

Alkynes

Unsaturated hydrocarbons containing at least one carbon-carbon triple bond. General formula: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂.

Preparation

  • From Calcium Carbide: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + C₂H₂ (Ethyne).
  • Dehydrohalogenation of vicinal dihalides: Requires strong bases.
    Step 1: Alc. KOH removes one HX.
    Step 2: NaNH₂ (stronger base) removes the second HX.

Properties & Reactions

  • Acidity of Terminal Alkynes: The sp hybridized carbon has 50% s-character, making it highly electronegative. The C-H bond is polar, and the proton can be easily removed by strong bases like NaNH₂.
  • Addition of H₂O (Hydration): Occurs in the presence of Hg²⁺/H⁺. Initially forms an enol, which undergoes tautomerization to form a more stable ketone (or acetaldehyde from ethyne).
  • Addition of HX: Follows Markownikoff's rule. With 2 moles of HX, geminal dihalides are formed.
  • Hydrogenation:
    • Lindlar's Catalyst (Pd/CaCO₃, poisoned with quinoline) stops at cis-alkene.
    • Birch Reduction (Na in liquid NH₃) yields trans-alkene.

⭐ Key Points

  • Terminal alkynes are acidic due to sp hybridization.
  • Hydration of alkynes yields ketones (except ethyne → acetaldehyde) via keto-enol tautomerization.
  • Stereoselective hydrogenation: Lindlar's gives cis, Birch gives trans.

Aromatic Hydrocarbons

Aromatic compounds (arenes) are highly stable cyclic compounds. Benzene (C₆H₆) is the parent compound.

Aromaticity (Hückel's Rule)

For a compound to be aromatic, it must be:

  1. Cyclic and planar.
  2. Completely conjugated (uninterrupted π cloud).
  3. Contain (4n + 2) π electrons, where n = 0, 1, 2...

Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS)

Because the benzene ring is electron-rich, it undergoes substitution by electrophiles while maintaining its stable aromatic system.

🔬 Reaction: EAS Halogenation (Bromination)
Overall: Benzene + Br₂ (with FeBr₃) → Bromobenzene + HBr
Br₂ + FeBr₃ → Br⁺ + FeBr₄⁻
Step 1: Generation of Electrophile
FeBr₃ acts as a Lewis acid and accepts a lone pair from Br₂, polarizing the bond and creating a strong bromonium ion electrophile (Br⁺).
Why? Benzene's π cloud is stable. A weak electrophile (like neutral Br₂) won't react. A Lewis acid catalyst is required to generate a strong electrophile.
C₆H₆ + Br⁺ → C₆H₆Br⁺ (Sigma Complex / Arenium Ion)
Step 2: Electrophilic Attack
Two π electrons from the benzene ring attack the Br⁺, forming a resonance-stabilized carbocation (sigma complex). Aromaticity is temporarily lost.
Why? The electron-rich ring is attracted to the positive electrophile.
C₆H₆Br⁺ + FeBr₄⁻ → C₆H₅Br + HBr + FeBr₃
Step 3: Loss of Proton (Rearomatization)
A base (FeBr₄⁻) removes a proton from the sp³ hybridized carbon. The electrons form a new π bond, restoring aromaticity.
Why? Regaining the massive stability of the aromatic system drives this final step. The catalyst (FeBr₃) is regenerated.
Step 1 of 3
🔬 Reaction: EAS Nitration
Overall: Benzene + HNO₃/H₂SO₄ → Nitrobenzene + H₂O
HNO₃ + H₂SO₄ ⇌ H₂NO₃⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → NO₂⁺ + H₂O + HSO₄⁻
Step 1: Generation of Nitronium Ion
Sulfuric acid (stronger acid) protonates nitric acid, which then loses water to form the nitronium ion (NO₂⁺) electrophile.
Why? The mixed acid generates a very strong electrophile needed to break benzene's stability.
C₆H₆ + NO₂⁺ → C₆H₆NO₂⁺
Step 2: Formation of Sigma Complex
The benzene ring attacks the NO₂⁺, losing aromaticity and forming the resonance-stabilized intermediate.
Why? The electron-rich ring seeks the strong positive charge on the nitrogen.
C₆H₆NO₂⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂SO₄
Step 3: Rearomatization
HSO₄⁻ removes the proton, restoring the aromatic ring to form nitrobenzene.
Why? Restoring aromatic stability. H₂SO₄ acts as a catalyst and is regenerated.
Step 1 of 3
🔬 Reaction: Friedel-Crafts Reactions
Overall: Alkylation (R-Cl) or Acylation (R-COCl) with AlCl₃
R-Cl + AlCl₃ → R⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ (Alkylation)
R-COCl + AlCl₃ → R-C≡O⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ (Acylation)
Step 1: Generation of Electrophile
The Lewis acid AlCl₃ abstracts a chloride ion to form a carbocation (alkylation) or acylium ion (acylation).
Why? To generate a strong carbon-based electrophile capable of attacking the aromatic ring. Note: In alkylation, the carbocation can undergo rearrangement to a more stable form before attacking!
Benzene + R⁺ → Arenium Ion → Substituted Benzene + H⁺
Step 2 & 3: Attack and Rearomatization
The aromatic ring attacks the carbocation/acylium ion, forms the sigma complex, and then loses a proton to restore aromaticity.
Why? Standard EAS mechanism driven by the ultimate restoration of aromatic stability.

⭐ Key Points

  • Aromatic compounds must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated, and follow Hückel's Rule (4n+2).
  • They primarily undergo Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS) to preserve the stable π system.
  • Activating groups direct ortho/para; deactivating groups direct meta (except halogens which are o/p directing but deactivating).
