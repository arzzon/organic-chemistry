Biomolecules

Structure, properties, and functions of carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids, and vitamins.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds which produce such units on hydrolysis.

Classification based on Hydrolysis

  1. Monosaccharides: Simplest carbohydrates, cannot be hydrolysed further (e.g., Glucose, Fructose, Ribose).
  2. Oligosaccharides: Yield 2-10 monosaccharide units on hydrolysis (e.g., Sucrose, Maltose, Lactose are disaccharides).
  3. Polysaccharides: Yield a large number of monosaccharide units (e.g., Starch, Cellulose, Glycogen).
⭐ NEET Tip

Reducing sugars: Have free aldehyde or ketone groups (reduce Fehling's/Tollens' reagent). All monosaccharides, maltose, lactose.
Non-reducing sugars: Do not have free aldehyde/ketone groups. Sucrose.

Monosaccharides

Glucose (Aldohexose): Structure elucidated by specific reactions:

  • HI/Δ: Forms n-hexane (straight chain of 6 carbons).
  • NH₂OH: Forms oxime (presence of carbonyl group).
  • Br₂ water: Oxidizes to gluconic acid (aldehyde group present).
  • Acetic anhydride: Forms pentaacetate (presence of 5 -OH groups).

Cyclic Structure: Exists in two cyclic hemiacetal forms (α and β anomers) represented by Haworth structures.

Fructose (Ketohexose): Has a ketone functional group at C-2. Forms a 5-membered furanose ring in cyclic form.

Disaccharides (Joined by glycosidic linkage)

  • Sucrose: α-D-glucose and β-D-fructose (C1-C2 linkage). Non-reducing sugar.
  • Maltose: Two α-D-glucose units (C1-C4 linkage). Reducing sugar.
  • Lactose: β-D-galactose and β-D-glucose (C1-C4 linkage). Reducing sugar.

Polysaccharides

  • Starch: Plant storage polymer of α-glucose. Two components: Amylose (water-soluble, linear, C1-C4) and Amylopectin (water-insoluble, branched, C1-C4 and C1-C6).
  • Cellulose: Plant cell wall component. Linear polymer of β-D-glucose (C1-C4 linkage).
  • Glycogen: Animal starch. Highly branched polymer of α-glucose.

⭐ Key Points

  • Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes/ketones.
  • Glucose open chain confirmed by HI, NH₂OH, Br₂ water.
  • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, while Maltose and Lactose are reducing.
  • Starch = Amylose + Amylopectin; Cellulose = β-glucose polymer.

Proteins and Enzymes

Proteins are polymers of α-amino acids connected by peptide bonds.

Amino Acids

  • Contain amino (-NH₂) and carboxyl (-COOH) functional groups.
  • Zwitterion: At a specific pH (isoelectric point), the -COOH loses a proton and -NH₂ gains a proton, forming a neutral dipolar ion.
  • Essential amino acids: Cannot be synthesized in the body (e.g., Valine, Leucine).
  • Non-essential amino acids: Synthesized in the body (e.g., Glycine, Alanine).
🔬 Reaction: Peptide Bond Formation
Amino Acid 1 + Amino Acid 2 → Dipeptide + H₂O
H₂N-CH(R₁)-COOH + H₂N-CH(R₂)-COOH
Step 1: Alignment of molecules
The carboxyl group of one amino acid aligns with the amino group of another.
Why? This orientation allows for a condensation reaction between the acid and amine functional groups.
H₂N-CH(R₁)-CO-NH-CH(R₂)-COOH + H₂O
Step 2: Condensation and Elimination of Water
The -OH from the carboxyl group and -H from the amino group are removed as water (H₂O), forming an amide (-CO-NH-) bond.
Why? This is an acid-catalyzed condensation forming the strong covalent peptide bond linking amino acids.
Step 1 of 2

Structure of Proteins

  1. Primary (1°): Specific sequence of amino acids.
  2. Secondary (2°): Folding of the polypeptide chain into α-helix or β-pleated sheet via hydrogen bonding.
  3. Tertiary (3°): Overall 3D folding due to H-bonds, disulfide linkages, van der Waals, and electrostatic forces.
  4. Quaternary (4°): Spatial arrangement of two or more polypeptide subunits.
⭐ NEET Tip

The primary structure dictates all higher-order structures. Even a single amino acid change (like in sickle cell anemia) can drastically alter protein function.

Classification & Denaturation

  • Fibrous Proteins: Polypeptide chains run parallel, held by H-bonds/disulfide bonds. Insoluble in water (e.g., Keratin, Myosin).
  • Globular Proteins: Chains fold into a spherical shape. Soluble in water (e.g., Insulin, Albumin).
  • Denaturation: Changes in pH or temperature disrupt 2° and 3° structures (1° structure remains intact), causing loss of biological activity (e.g., boiling an egg).

Enzymes

Biocatalysts (mostly globular proteins) that are highly specific. They operate via the lock-and-key model where the substrate fits precisely into the active site.

⭐ Key Points

  • Amino acids exist as zwitterions at their isoelectric point.
  • Proteins are linked by peptide (-CO-NH-) bonds.
  • Protein structures range from primary (sequence) to quaternary (multiple subunits).
  • Denaturation destroys 2° and 3° structure, keeping 1° intact.

Nucleic Acids

Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are biopolymers of nucleotides responsible for the transmission of genetic information and protein synthesis.

Components of Nucleic Acids

  • Pentose Sugar: β-D-ribose in RNA, β-D-2-deoxyribose in DNA.
  • Phosphoric Acid Group: Links sugars via phosphodiester linkage.
  • Nitrogenous Bases:
    • Purines: Adenine (A), Guanine (G).
    • Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), Thymine (T, only DNA), Uracil (U, only RNA).
ℹ️ Nucleoside vs Nucleotide

Nucleoside = Sugar + Base (attached at 1' position).
Nucleotide = Sugar + Base + Phosphate (attached at 5' position of sugar).

DNA Structure

James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double helix model. Two polynucleotide chains run anti-parallel and are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs:

  • Adenine pairs with Thymine (A=T, 2 H-bonds)
  • Guanine pairs with Cytosine (G≡C, 3 H-bonds)
⭐ NEET Tip

Chargaff's Rule: In double-stranded DNA, the amount of Purines equals the amount of Pyrimidines. (A + G = C + T).

Types of RNA and Central Dogma

  • mRNA (messenger): Carries genetic code from DNA to ribosomes.
  • rRNA (ribosomal): Structural component of ribosomes.
  • tRNA (transfer): Brings amino acids to the ribosome during protein synthesis.

Central Dogma: DNA → (Transcription) → RNA → (Translation) → Protein

Nucleic Acid Replication

DNA replication is semi-conservative; the two strands separate and each acts as a template for synthesizing a new complementary strand.

Feature DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) RNA (Ribonucleic Acid)
Sugar β-D-2-deoxyribose β-D-ribose
Pyrimidines Cytosine (C) and Thymine (T) Cytosine (C) and Uracil (U)
Structure Double-stranded helix Single-stranded (usually)
Function Stores genetic information Protein synthesis

⭐ Key Points

  • Nucleotide = Sugar + Base + Phosphate.
  • DNA contains A, G, C, T; RNA contains A, G, C, U.
  • DNA is a double helix with A=T and G≡C base pairing.
  • Central Dogma describes information flow from DNA to Protein.

Vitamins and Hormones

Vitamins are essential organic compounds required in small amounts in our diet for normal growth, maintenance, and biological functions.

Classification of Vitamins

  • Fat-Soluble: Vitamins A, D, E, K. Stored in liver and adipose tissues.
  • Water-Soluble: Vitamins B-complex and C. Must be supplied regularly as they are excreted in urine (except B₁₂).

Hormones (Brief Overview)

Hormones are intercellular messengers produced by endocrine glands and poured directly into the bloodstream to regulate biological processes. Examples include Insulin (regulates blood sugar), Thyroxine (metabolism), and Epinephrine.

⭐ Key Points

  • Vitamins A, D, E, K are fat-soluble; B, C are water-soluble.
  • Vitamin B₁₂ is the only water-soluble vitamin that is stored in the body.
  • Deficiency of Vitamin C causes Scurvy, Vitamin A causes Night blindness.
  • Hormones act as chemical messengers from endocrine glands.
